Module 11. Discovery, ROI and proposals

Technical skill gets you a pilot only if the client trusts your numbers. This module turns discovery into a repeatable product: a two-week readiness audit, a scored list of use cases, an ROI model that survives a sceptical CFO, and a proposal the client can sign.

By the end of this module you can

  • Run a fixed-scope, two-week AI readiness audit and say what the client receives at the end

  • Place a company on the five-step maturity ladder and defend the placement with evidence
  • Score use cases on value, feasibility and risk with a weighted sheet, and apply a risk gate

11.1 The readiness audit as your entry offer

A small company does not buy "AI transformation". It buys a clear answer to "what should we do first, what will it return, and what could go wrong?". The readiness audit sells that answer for a fixed scope and a short time. It is also the cheapest way for you to learn whether the client is a good fit before you commit to a build.

The audit is the design stage of Vastama's design, build, test, monitor pipeline, packaged so a client can buy it alone. Done well, it sells the build stage for you, because the pilot is already specified and costed.

WhenActivityOutput
Before day 1Kick-off call, signed SOW, named sponsor, interview slots booked, data request sentInterview schedule, access list
Week 16 to 10 stakeholder interviews; swimlane mapping of 2 or 3 processes; data and systems inventory; start measuring baselinesInterview notes, current-state swimlanes, inventory
Week 2Maturity scoring; long list of use cases scored; ROI for the top 2 or 3; risk and governance notes; report; readout meetingAudit report, scoring sheet, ROI workbook, pilot proposal

What makes it a product and not a favour: a fixed scope, a fixed number of interviews, named outputs, and a readout where the sponsor makes a decision. What stays out: building anything, connecting to production systems, or touching personal data beyond samples the client chooses to share.

11.2 Stakeholder interviews

Interviews are where you find the real work. Ask about the last time something happened, not about how things usually go. Ask to see the artefacts: the spreadsheet, the inbox, the form. Forty-five minutes per person; two people from the same function if the team is large. Interview in the language the person works in (Hebrew, Russian or English) and take notes in English.

FunctionQuestion bank (pick 6 to 8)
Owner / CEOWhat decision do you make with the least information? What would you want to see every Monday? What have you already tried with AI? What data must never leave the company? What would make this project a failure for you?
FinanceHow many invoices, payments and reminders per month? Walk me through the last invoice from receipt to payment. Which tasks are done at month end under pressure? Where do errors come from and what do they cost?
SalesHow does a quote request arrive and how long until it is answered? What gets forgotten? What do reps write in the CRM, and what do they skip? Which customers get the slowest replies?
OperationsHow do you find out a shipment is late? Who chases suppliers and how? Which spreadsheets run the business? What breaks when one person is away?
Customer serviceWhat are the five most common questions? Where do the answers live? How often do you escalate and why? Which languages do customers write in?
MarketingWhich content is missing or out of date? Who translates, and how long does it take? How do you reuse supplier material?
HRHow many roles and CVs per year? Which documents do you write again and again? What personal data do you hold and where?
IT / systemsWhich systems hold customers, stock and finance? Can they export data, and in what format? Who has admin rights? Is there a data policy, a backup, a Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace admin?
Always ask these closing questions: "What did I not ask that I should have?", "Who else should I talk to?", "What worries you about AI here?", "If we could save you three hours a week, which three hours?"

11.3 Process mapping and the data and systems inventory

For each of the two or three processes that came up most in interviews, draw a current-state swimlane: one lane per actor (person, team or system), steps left to right, and a note on each step for volume, time and pain. Do it on a whiteboard or in a shared document with the process owner in the room. Do not draw the future state yet; the point is to see where time and errors accumulate.

Connected data is the step that most often blocks SMBs. A system with an export is enough for a pilot; a system with no export means the first project is the export.

11.4 Maturity scoring: the five-step ladder

The ladder gives the owner a picture of where they are and what "better" means, without jargon. Score the company as a whole and each department that matters, with evidence from interviews.

StepWhat it looks likeEvidence you look forTypical next move
1. Ad hoc chatIndividuals use chat tools on their own, no rulesPersonal accounts, no policy, no shared promptsUsage policy, team workspace
2. Team workspaceShared team plan, shared Projects and prompts, a usage policyAdmin console in use, a few champions, saved templatesConnect the first data source
3. Connected dataClaude reads company data through approved connectorsConnectors or MCP in place, data classes agreedAutomate one reviewed workflow
4. Automated flowsSpecific workflows run on triggers, a person reviews exceptionsLogs, evals, an owner per workflow, cost trackingAdd monitoring and a change process
5. AI-native opsNew processes are designed around AI from the start, with continuous measurementGovernance in routine use, metrics reviewed monthlyExpand by function; keep evaluating models

Score four dimensions from 1 to 5 each: people and skills, data and systems, process clarity, governance. The overall step is the lowest of the four, rounded down, because the weakest dimension limits what you can safely build. A company with a good team but no usable data is at step 1 or 2, whatever the enthusiasm.

11.5 Use-case scoring: value, feasibility, risk

Collect 10 to 15 candidate use cases from interviews. Score each 1 to 5 on three questions, with the client in the room for value and risk.

CriterionQuestion1 means5 means
ValueHow much time, error cost or revenue is at stake?A few hours a monthLarge share of a team's week, or a clear revenue effect
FeasibilityIs the data reachable, the task text-based and checkable, the owner willing?No data access, vague taskData exportable now, clear right answer, eager owner
RiskWhat happens if the output is wrong or leaks?Internal draft, easily correctedMoney moves, legal exposure, personal data, no review step

Combine them in a weighted sheet. Risk is inverted so a higher total is always better: score = w_v × value + w_f × feasibility + w_r × (6 − risk). A starting set of weights is 0.45, 0.35 and 0.20, but agree the weights with the client before you score, so the ranking is theirs. Then apply a gate: risk 4 or 5, or feasibility below 3, is never the first pilot, whatever the total.

Do not sell the top score. Sell the top score that also passes the gate and has a measurable baseline. A use case with no baseline cannot be proven, so it cannot become a retainer.

11.6 The ROI model

A credible ROI model is boring and conservative. It has five parts.

  1. Baseline first. Measure what happens today before you promise anything: items per month, minutes per item, errors per month, cost per error. Use a two-week sample, a log, or a timesheet extract. If you cannot measure, say "estimate" next to the number and widen the range.
  2. Time value. Hours saved per month × loaded cost per hour. Loaded cost is salary plus employer costs and overhead, from the client's finance manager. Hours saved = baseline hours × share of time removed × adoption.
  3. Error reduction. Errors avoided × average cost to fix one. Include rework time and the cost of a customer complaint only if the client can show it.
  4. Revenue lift. Faster quotes or follow-ups can raise margin, but count it only when you can name the mechanism and the number. Default to zero in the low case.
  5. Run costs. Licences, API usage, maintenance and monitoring time (yours or theirs). Take licence and API prices from the current pricing pages on the day you quote, and write that date in the model.

Then compute net benefit per month = time value + error value + revenue lift − run costs, and payback period = one-off cost ÷ net monthly benefit. Show it as low, base and high so the client sees the range, not a single hopeful number.

Sensitivity and honesty

  • Vary only the inputs that are uncertain: share of time removed, adoption, error reduction, API usage. Leave measured inputs alone.
  • If the low case never pays back, say so. Offer a smaller pilot, a gate after two weeks, or a different use case. A client who sees the low case in your model will trust the base case.
  • Time saved is not cash saved. State where the freed hours go: more quotes answered, faster month end, no new hire. If the answer is "nowhere", the benefit is soft; label it soft.
  • Re-measure at the end of the pilot with the same method as the baseline. That comparison is what you show at the retainer conversation.

11.7 Packaging: audit, pilot, retainer

OfferScopeClient getsEnds when
Audit (2 weeks)Interviews, mapping, inventory, scoring, ROI, reportRanked use cases, ROI range, pilot proposalReadout held, decision made
Pilot (4 to 6 weeks)One use case, small user group, baseline and end measurement, trainingWorking workflow, evals, measured result, handover notesAcceptance criteria met or not, decision on scaling

The retainer is Vastama's monitor stage sold as a service. It is the part clients underestimate, because models, prices, data and staff change. Sell it as keeping the result alive, with a monthly report showing the same metrics you measured in the pilot.

Pricing approaches

This course does not give prices; market rates change and depend on your positioning. Choose the approach by how well the work is defined.

ApproachHow it worksFits whenMain risk
Fixed scopeOne price for named deliverables and acceptance criteriaAudit, pilot, any well-defined buildScope creep eats your margin; control with out-of-scope list and change control
Time and materialsHourly or daily rate, with an estimate and a cap or review pointUnclear integrations, exploratory workClient fears an open bill; you carry no overrun risk but lose trust if unmanaged
Value-basedFee tied to measured benefit (share of savings, or price anchored to a business outcome)Baseline is solid and the client trusts the measurementDisputes over measurement; benefits may come late. Agree the metric and method in the SOW first

A common sequence: fixed scope for audit and pilot, then a monthly retainer, with value-based elements only after a measured pilot. Whatever you choose, state what is included, what is billed separately (for example licences and API usage, which belong to the client's account), and when prices are reviewed.

11.8 Writing the SOW

A statement of work is a short contract-backed description of what you will do. Its job is to prevent the argument you can already see coming. Seven parts do most of the work.

PartWhat to writeCommon mistake
ScopeDeliverables as nouns, with quantities (number of interviews, workflows, users, languages)Verbs like "support" or "optimise" with no object
Out of scopeThings a reasonable client might expect that you will not doLeaving it empty
AssumptionsConditions the plan depends on: data available by date X, a named reviewer, a plan with required featuresHiding them in the body text
Acceptance criteriaMeasurable tests on real samples: accuracy on a sample set, time per item, review process"To the client's satisfaction"
Client responsibilitiesPeople, decisions and access the client must provide, with response timesNot saying what happens if they are late
Data accessWhich data, which systems, read-only or not, who approves, where it is processed, retention and deletionAsking for full access "to be safe"
Change controlHow new requests are estimated and approved in writingVerbal agreements

11.9 Change management

Most pilots that fail technically work; most that fail in practice were never adopted. Plan the human side with the same discipline as the build.

ElementWhat you doAdoption signal
ChampionsOne respected person per team, given time and a direct line to you. They test early, collect complaints and teach peers.Champion keeps using it after week 2
TrainingShort role-based sessions on the team's own tasks, in their language, followed by a one-page guide and office hoursTasks per user per week rising
CommunicationsOwner announces why, what changes, what does not, and how people can raise concerns. Keep a short FAQ in Hebrew and Russian.Fewer rumours; questions arrive through the agreed channel
Adoption metricsWeekly active users, tasks completed, share of outputs accepted without edit, time per task vs baselineReviewed at weeks 2, 4 and 6 of the pilot
Feedback loopLog errors and requests; fix the top item each week and say soComplaints shift from "it is wrong" to "can it also do X"

11.10 Objection handling

Objections are information. Treat each as a request for evidence, not a challenge to answer cleverly.

ObjectionWhat is behind itResponseEvidence to bring
"It costs too much"Fear of paying for nothing, unclear valueStart with the fixed-scope audit; show the low case; set a stop gate in the pilotYour ROI sheet with baseline and assumptions
"What about our data and privacy?"Customer lists, prices, employee data leaving the companyClassify data first; use the least data needed; choose the plan by its current terms; no personal data in the pilot unless approvedData inventory, current terms and Trust Center pages with the date you checked, your governance work from Module 10
"AI makes mistakes"Past bad experience, or fear of blameAgreed: so a person reviews, and we measure accuracy on your own samples against the human error rate you have todayAcceptance criteria, a sample test run on their documents
"It will take our jobs"Staff and owner worries; resistance from the people you needWe change tasks, not roles; say where freed time goes; involve staff in choosing what to automate. Never promise either "no change" or "fewer people"Champion programme, task-level maps showing the dull work being removed

score_usecases.py (weighted scoring, risk gate, chart)

import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")  # no display needed
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

from audit_data import USE_CASES

WEIGHTS = {"value": 0.45, "feasibility": 0.35, "risk": 0.20}  # agree these with the client first
assert abs(sum(WEIGHTS.values()) - 1.0) < 1e-9

def build_sheet() -> pd.DataFrame:
    cols = ["id", "name", "function", "value", "feasibility", "risk", "hours"]
    df = pd.DataFrame(USE_CASES, columns=cols)
    # risk is inverted so that a higher number is always better
    df["risk_inv"] = 6 - df["risk"]
    df["score"] = (
        WEIGHTS["value"] * df["value"]
        + WEIGHTS["feasibility"] * df["feasibility"]
        + WEIGHTS["risk"] * df["risk_inv"]
    ).round(2)
    # gate: risk 4-5 or feasibility below 3 is never a first pilot, whatever its score
    df["pilot_ok"] = (df["risk"] <= 3) & (df["feasibility"] >= 3)
    df = df.sort_values("score", ascending=False).reset_index(drop=True)
    df["rank"] = df.index + 1
    return df

def plot_matrix(df: pd.DataFrame, path: str = "usecase_matrix.png") -> None:
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
    colors = df["risk"].map(lambda r: "#c8642c" if r >= 4 else "#0f7a72")
    ax.scatter(df["feasibility"], df["value"], s=df["hours"] * 4 + 40, c=colors, alpha=0.75)
    for _, r in df.iterrows():
        ax.annotate(r["id"], (r["feasibility"], r["value"]), xytext=(6, 6), textcoords="offset points")
    ax.axhline(3.5, color="grey", lw=0.8)
    ax.axvline(3.5, color="grey", lw=0.8)
    ax.set_xlim(0.5, 5.5)
    ax.set_ylim(0.5, 5.5)
    ax.set_xlabel("Feasibility (1-5)")
    ax.set_ylabel("Value (1-5)")
    ax.set_title("Use cases: value vs feasibility (size = baseline hours, orange = high risk)")
    fig.tight_layout()
    fig.savefig(path, dpi=130)
    plt.close(fig)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    sheet = build_sheet()
    pd.set_option("display.width", 140)
    pd.set_option("display.max_colwidth", 52)
    print(sheet[["rank", "id", "name", "value", "feasibility", "risk", "score", "pilot_ok"]].to_string(index=False))
    sheet.to_csv("usecase_scores.csv", index=False)
    plot_matrix(sheet)
    print("\nSaved usecase_scores.csv and usecase_matrix.png")

roi_calc.py (ROI calculator with scenarios and payback)

from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Case:
    name: str
    baseline_hours: float          # MEASURED hours per month on this task today
    loaded_cost_hour: float        # salary + employer costs + overhead, per hour (ILS)
    time_saved_share: float        # share of baseline hours removed at full adoption
    adoption: float                # share of the team actually using it
    errors_per_month: float        # MEASURED errors or rework items today
    cost_per_error: float          # average cost to fix one (ILS)
    error_reduction: float         # share of errors removed
    revenue_lift_month: float      # extra gross margin per month, 0 if you cannot defend it
    seats: int                     # paid licences for this use case
    seat_cost_month: float         # PLACEHOLDER: copy from the current plan page on the day you quote
    api_cost_month: float          # estimate from tokens per item x items x current API pricing
    maintenance_hours_month: float # your retainer time or client time
    maintenance_cost_hour: float
    one_off_cost: float            # audit share + pilot build + training (ILS)

# Multipliers applied to the "base" case. Low = cautious, high = good adoption.
SCENARIOS = {
    "low":  {"time_saved_share": 0.60, "adoption": 0.70, "error_reduction": 0.50,
             "revenue_lift_month": 0.0, "api_cost_month": 1.5},
    "base": {"time_saved_share": 1.00, "adoption": 1.00, "error_reduction": 1.00,
             "revenue_lift_month": 1.0, "api_cost_month": 1.0},
    "high": {"time_saved_share": 1.25, "adoption": 1.10, "error_reduction": 1.20,
             "revenue_lift_month": 1.5, "api_cost_month": 0.9},
}

def run(case: Case, scenario: str) -> dict:
    m = SCENARIOS[scenario]
    share = min(case.time_saved_share * m["time_saved_share"], 0.9)
    adoption = min(case.adoption * m["adoption"], 1.0)
    hours_saved = case.baseline_hours * share * adoption
    time_value = hours_saved * case.loaded_cost_hour
    err_red = min(case.error_reduction * m["error_reduction"], 0.95)
    error_value = case.errors_per_month * err_red * case.cost_per_error
    revenue = case.revenue_lift_month * m["revenue_lift_month"]
    gross = time_value + error_value + revenue
    run_cost = (case.seats * case.seat_cost_month
                + case.api_cost_month * m["api_cost_month"]
                + case.maintenance_hours_month * case.maintenance_cost_hour)
    net = gross - run_cost
    payback = case.one_off_cost / net if net > 0 else float("inf")
    return {
        "scenario": scenario,
        "hours_saved": round(hours_saved, 1),
        "time_value": round(time_value),
        "error_value": round(error_value),
        "revenue": round(revenue),
        "gross_month": round(gross),
        "run_cost_month": round(run_cost),
        "net_month": round(net),
        "payback_months": round(payback, 1) if payback != float("inf") else None,
        "net_year_1": round(net * 12 - case.one_off_cost),
    }

def table(case: Case):
    import pandas as pd
    return pd.DataFrame([run(case, s) for s in SCENARIOS]).set_index("scenario")

report_outline.py (assembles the audit report outline)

import pandas as pd
from audit_data import INTERVIEWS
from score_usecases import build_sheet, WEIGHTS
from roi_calc import UC01, UC02, run

def outline() -> str:
    sheet = build_sheet()
    shortlist = sheet[sheet["pilot_ok"]].head(3)
    lines = ["# AI readiness audit: Negev Import & Distribution Ltd", ""]
    lines += ["## 1. Executive summary (one page)",
              "- Maturity today: step 1 of 5, ad hoc chat. Target in 12 months: step 3, connected data.",
              f"- Recommended pilot: {shortlist.iloc[0]['name']}",
              "- Decision requested: approve a 4-6 week pilot", ""]
    lines += ["## 2. What we did",
              f"- {len(INTERVIEWS)} stakeholder interviews ({', '.join(i['function'] for i in INTERVIEWS)})",
              "- Current-state swimlane for invoice-to-ERP and quote-to-order",
              "- Data and systems inventory, maturity scoring", ""]
    lines += ["## 3. Maturity scoring (table: 5-step ladder, current step, evidence)", ""]
    lines += ["## 4. Use-case ranking",
              f"- Weights: {WEIGHTS}", "- Table of 12 use cases, 2x2 chart, gate: risk 4-5 or feasibility under 3 is never a first pilot"]
    for _, r in shortlist.iterrows():
        lines.append(f"- Shortlist: {r['id']} {r['name']} (score {r['score']})")
    lines.append("")
    lines += ["## 5. ROI for the shortlist (low / base / high, baseline measured, assumptions listed)"]
    for c in (UC01, UC02):
        b, lo = run(c, "base"), run(c, "low")
        lines.append(f"- {c.name}: base net {b['net_month']} ILS/month, payback {b['payback_months']} months "
                     f"(low case {lo['payback_months']} months)")
    lines.append("")
    lines += ["## 6. Risks, privacy and governance (data classes, who sees what, human review points)", "",
              "## 7. Roadmap: pilot (4-6 weeks), then retainer (monitor and improve)", "",
              "## 8. Change plan: champions, training, communications, adoption metrics", "",
              "## 9. Next steps and SOW reference", "",
              "## Appendix: interview notes, scoring sheet, ROI workbook, data inventory"]
    return "\n".join(lines)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    text = outline()
    print(text)
    with open("audit_report_outline.md", "w", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        f.write(text)

Self-check

  1. Why do you measure the baseline before you build the ROI model, and what do you do if the client cannot provide one?
    Model answerWithout a baseline you cannot prove any improvement, and the model rests on guesses. If none exists, take a short sample (two weeks of logs or a timed sample of items), label the figures as estimates, widen the low-to-high range, and make baseline measurement the first task of the pilot.
  2. A use case has the highest total score but risk 5 (it changes prices in the ERP without review). What do you do?
    Model answerApply the gate: risk 4 or 5 is not a first pilot. Propose a lower-risk step on the same process, for example Claude proposes price changes and a person approves them, and plan governance work before any autonomy. Keep the full-automation version on the roadmap.
  3. In the lab, the low case pays back in more than five years. What do you tell the client, and what do you change?
    Model answerShow it openly: the project depends on adoption and time saved. Change the design to protect it: smaller scope, a two-week checkpoint with a stop option, a training plan to raise adoption, lower fixed costs, or choose a use case with stronger value. A visible low case builds trust in the base case.
  4. Name three items that belong in "out of scope" for a pilot SOW, and the section that handles a client request for more.
    Model answerFor example: connecting to production systems beyond the agreed read access, supporting additional languages or departments, training every employee, ongoing monitoring after handover (that is the retainer). New requests go through change control: written estimate, written approval, adjusted timeline and fee.
  5. An owner says: "AI makes mistakes, and my staff are afraid for their jobs." Give a response that deals with both without overpromising.
    Model answerAgree that mistakes happen and show how you control them: human review, accuracy measured on their own samples against today's human error rate, acceptance criteria. On jobs, say the pilot changes specific tasks, say where freed time will go, involve staff and champions in the design, and do not promise either outcome on headcount.
